Dr. Wei-Cheng Chen is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Molecular Science and Engineering at National Taipei University of Technology. His research lies at the intersection of soft electronic materials, perovskite semiconductors, optoelectronic devices, and neuromorphic electronics, with a particular emphasis on ultralow-energy operation, mechanical adaptability, and bio-inspired functionality.

He received his Ph.D. in Chemical Engineering from National Taiwan University in 2025, following earlier training at NTUT for both his B.S. and M.S. degrees. His interdisciplinary background spans conjugated polymer design, perovskite quantum dots, nanoscale morphology control, and device physics, allowing him to bridge molecular-level materials engineering with system-level device architectures. He has authored more than 40 peer-reviewed publications, with multiple first-author contributions in Materials Horizons, Chemical Engineering Journal, and Materials Today.

The core philosophy of the group is that materials design should be guided by device function and system demands. The lab develops hybrid polymer–perovskite platforms for flexible photosynaptic transistors, photodetectors, stretchable optoelectronics, and neuromorphic computing, aiming to overcome the energy and scalability limits of conventional AI hardware. Beyond synthesis, the work places strong emphasis on structure–property–function relationships, combining advanced characterisation with device-level modelling.

In parallel with research, Dr. Chen is committed to mentorship and laboratory culture, emphasising critical thinking, experimental iteration, and scientific communication as the pillars of training for young researchers.

Materials × Process × Devices

Research Interests

Soft optoelectronic and organic / organic–inorganic hybrid semiconductor materials.

Since polymer semiconductor research was recognised by the Nobel Prize in 2000, charge carrier mobility in polymer semiconductors has passed 10 cm²/V·s — rivalling silicon, while keeping mechanical flexibility, solution processability, low cost, and large-area fabrication. The open challenge is reaching that performance and keeping the process compatible and scalable. Our work runs across three connected directions.

01Materials Science

We focus on molecular design, doping strategies, energy-level engineering, and nanoscale morphology control of soft semiconductor materials. By systematically investigating the relationships among molecular structure, nanoscale morphology, optical properties, and electrical behaviour, we aim to establish fundamental structure–property correlations — in particular the light–charge–material interaction mechanisms, combining optical characterisation, charge carrier dynamics, energy-level modulation, and interfacial engineering into a physics-based understanding that links material design to device function.

02Process Engineering

We develop solution-processable thin-film technologies for polymer and hybrid semiconductor materials, including phase separation behaviour, process optimisation, and interfacial engineering. These efforts improve processing stability and reproducibility on large-area, low-temperature, and flexible substrates, making it realistic to translate laboratory-scale materials into practical applications.

03Advanced Electronic Devices

At the device level, our research extends to lasers, photodetectors, soft optoelectronic devices, and neuromorphic/synaptic optoelectronic devices. We place particular emphasis on ultralow-power operation, the integration of in-sensor and in-memory computing, and biomimetic electronic functionality for next-generation intelligent electronics and artificial neural systems.

Through the co-design of material synthesis, optoelectronic analysis, hybrid interfacial engineering, and device integration, the lab aims to overcome the energy-consumption and mechanical limitations of silicon-based electronics — and to establish the material design principles and device strategies for low-power, flexible, intelligent optoelectronic and neuromorphic systems.
